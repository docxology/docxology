Build a curious rocket: a hands-on continuous-state AIF
session with cpomdp
Presenter: Dan Corva, Kevin Backhouse
Contribution: Interactive Workshop
Abstract
In the 1960s, Rudolf Kalman wrote down a filter that would go on to navigate Apollo to the moon.
It has been the workhorse of continuous-state estimation ever since, and if you have ever built a
continuous active inference agent in the linear-Gaussian regime, you have built one too, whether
you intended to or not. Bolt a goal onto it and you get a very good controller that is not curious in
the slightest. Koudahl, Kouw and de Vries proved why in 2021: under a fixed sensor the epistemic
value is identical for every action, so expected free energy quietly reduces to Linear Quadratic
Regulator (LQR). In this workshop, in about ten lines of Python that will look familiar to anyone
who has used pymdp, we are going to build a rocket! Then, we add the interesting part, curiosity,
by making a surgical, biologically realistic change: adding sensor noise that varies based on the
rocket's position. We will see how the flight path bends when we add artifacts like a navigation
beacon or a blackout zone, and explore the science as to why. You will write every line yourself
in a Colab notebook and do not worry there will be plenty of scaffolding from the cpomdp team.
Code hints will drop in on a timer, and a reference cell for anyone who gets stuck or would rather
just see the finished steps. We will build this in three steps: the 1960 rocket, a nav beacon it can
fix its position by, and a blackout zone. Everything we use runs on the PyPI release of cpomdp and
anything you build in the notebook is yours to keep and continue working on. Prerequisites: com-
fortable editing a few lines of Python. Familiarity with pymdp helps but is not required. Nothing
to install; a browser and a google account is enough. If you would like to use your own IDE feel
free but we may not be able to provide 1-on-1 support if you get stuck.

A Modular Perspective Layer for Active Inference Agents
Presenter: Hongju Pae
Contribution: Interactive Workshop
Abstract
Most agent architectures collapse two things that phenomenology keeps apart: what the agent
should do immediately, and what kind of world the agent takes itself to be in. This session intro-
duces “perspective” as a distinct architectural layer that carries the second. Perspective is a slowly
evolving, history-sensitive latent state that gates how observations are encoded. Importantly, it
is decoupled from policy gradients and is never itself an optimization target. Because it is decou-
pled, it is portable and can be attached on top of an existing policy-optimizing agent rather than
requiring a bespoke design. The first part of the session develops the conceptual frame: how a
phenomenologically motivated condition translates into a concrete architectural commitment, and
why that commitment is orthogonal to belief-state inference and policy selection. I then trace the
capabilities that this single layer has been shown to support across a sequence of studies, including
directional hysteresis, history-dependent perceptual reorganization, and body-grounded conative
orientation, treating these as unified aspects of a single portable module. There will then be a live
demonstration attaching the perspective layer to an active inference agent, such as a hierarchical
Bayesian / discrete state-space model, and running it in parallel with its unaugmented counterpart
in the same environment, so participants can see exactly what the layer adds and where it plugs
in. The session closes with a structured discussion in which participants are invited to identify
candidate insertion points for a perspective layer in their own active inference agents, whether
built in PyMDP, RxInfer, or custom frameworks. The aim is to leave participants able to reason
about whether and how a subjective perspective could be added.

Active Inference, Bayesian Causal Learning and Empower-
ment in RL.
Presenter: Alison Gopnik
Contribution: Livestreamed panel or group session
Abstract
Cognitive scientists have applied advances in our formal understanding of causation in philosophy
and computer science, particularly within the Causal Bayes Net formalism, to understand human
causal learning. This formalism is also closely related to ideas in active inference. In parallel,
reinforcement learning researchers have developed the idea of an intrinsic reward signal called
“empowerment”. An agent is rewarded for maximizing the mutual information between its actions
and their outcomes, regardless of the external reward value of those outcomes. “Empowerment”
may be an important bridge between classical Bayesian causal learning and reinforcement learning
and may help to characterize causal learning in humans and enable it in machines

Root Frequency Theory: An Integrative Framework for the
Continuity of Lived Experience
Presenter: Bianca Avanzo
Contribution: Livestreamed panel or group session
Abstract
How do we keep a coherent sense of self across time — and can that process be measured in
real time, on consumer hardware, before it breaks down? Root Frequency Theory (RFT) proposes
that experiential continuity is not a default state but an ongoing biological achievement requir-
ing coordination across five nested regulatory layers: environmental constraints (C0), autopoietic
regulation (C1), predictive neural integration (C2), narrative self-organization (C3), and the conti-
nuity of lived experience (C4). Fragmentation is defined as a self-reinforcing cascade of cross-scale
misalignment. The cascade is bidirectional: structured self-knowledge introduced at C3 — opera-
tionalized as epistemic alignment (Ke) — is hypothesized to initiate upward regulatory stabilization.
To test this, I developed the M-RFT metric: M = (Φ · Ke) / F, where Φ indexes autonomic flexibility
via HRV, Ke composites interoceptive accuracy and narrative coherence, and F captures regula-
tory burden. I then built Root Extension — an iOS application using iPhone and Apple Watch — to
administer this protocol ecologically and deliver personalized epistemic scaffolding upon session
completion. The N=1 longitudinal study ran 321 sessions across 23 active days (April 16–May
14, 2026), with the researcher as the sole subject. Key findings: mean M = 3.79 (Mdn = 2.14),
intra-session regulatory recovery — FRAGMENTED to RESILIENT within the same calendar day
— observed on 34.8% of active days. This presentation introduces the theoretical architecture, the
metric, the app protocol, and the preliminary data — and invites the active inference community
into the next phase: a formal N=20–40 validation trial planned for 2027.

CogNarr Story Graph: A Formal Semantics for Narrative as
a Belief Model
Presenter: John Boik, Glen EP Ropella
Contribution: Livestreamed panel or group session
Abstract
People naturally express their beliefs about a topic, situation, or problem in the form of narra-
tive. CogNarr is a soon-to-be-released, open-source, interactive tool that lets a writer transform
narrative into an explicit, formal semantic graph, which they can inspect and correct until it faith-
fully reflects their belief model. A CogNarr story graph captures a narrative's argument structure,
its timing, and the modality of its claims, including predictions and conditionals, together with
the uncertainty attached to each. In general it expresses the verifiable meaning of what a per-
son wishes to say, while reducing the ambiguity of natural language. As such, it could be of use
in modeling the beliefs that active-inference processes operate over, and as a framework within
which active-inference methods could be employed to infer semantic meaning from text. Because
it makes a person's beliefs explicit, transparent, and shareable, CogNarr has potential wherever
consequential beliefs are expressed as narrative, from medicine and governance to public polling
and accident reporting. As well, CogNarr story graphs support accurate agent-agent communica-
tion. The collective-cognition aim behind the project is alignment of shared belief models during
decision-making processes in the large-group setting. The talk will be a preview of features and
capabilities, with some discussion of potential applications.

From Self-Evidencing to Global Governance: Active Infer-
ence and the Dynamics of Justice
Presenter: Majid D. Beni
Contribution: Livestreamed panel or group session
Abstract
Active inference frames biological and cognitive agency as the imperatives of self-evidencing—
maintaining systemic integrity by minimising variational free energy within an open, stochas-
tic environment. Under this formalism, agents, from single cells to complex organisms, persist
by regulating their internal states against entropic dissipation, enacting a Markov blanket that
partitions self from world. This imperative entails sophisticated inference, balancing epistemic
drives against pragmatic preferences, thereby constituting adaptive agency.
This talk extends
this foundational picture of selfhood to the domain of political communities and global justice the-
ory, demonstrating that the same dynamical constraints that govern individual persistence scale
hierarchically to structure collective organisation and inter-state conduct. Drawing on the Free
Energy Principle, political communities are modelled as open, self-organising systems embedded
within a nested hierarchy of interacting Markov blankets. Their stability is not intrinsic but re-
lational, contingent upon continuous exchange of information, resources, and institutional con-
straints with their environment.
Within this multiscale architecture, global justice emerges as
an active regulatory imperative: a homeostatic constraint that maintains a nonequilibrium steady
state across coupled societies. We formalise this through the expected free energy decomposition,
where just policies balance risk—adherence to shared normative constraints such as human rights
and non-aggression—against ambiguity—the active resolution of uncertainty arising from struc-
tural interdependence. The normative implications are acute. We identify a pervasive pathology
of self-organisation, wherein dominant or insular political regimes mis-specify themselves as closed
systems. By suppressing external coupling and attenuating sensory evidence, they optimise local
coherence at the expense of systemic stability. Drawing parallels to oncogenic cellular prolifera-
tion and psychotic cognitive inference, we argue that such closure systematically underestimates
global risk and propagates destabilising volatility.
Ultimately, we contend that justice is not a
static distributive outcome nor a single-level contractual procedure, but the dynamic property of a
multiscale system that successfully synchronises its trajectories under persistent uncertainty. This
offers a unified, first-principles framework for linking the philosophy of agency to the normative
demands of a deeply interconnected global order.

A New Politics: From Linear to Sorted Politics Through
the Policy Map
Presenter: Montijn van Loo
Contribution: Livestreamed panel or group session
Affiliations: Tilburg University
ORCID: 0009-0003-0249-3240
Presenter background
I am an econometrist gone rogue. Instead of throwing mathematics at finance, I throw Active
Inference mathematics at philosophy. See for the consciousness, morality, politics, gender, free
will and God applications, with the poem uniting them all.
Abstract
Political disagreement often reflects competing worldviews: different beliefs about how the world
works and what will happen under alternative policies.
Yet political debate proceeds linearly,
through proposals, responses, and counterresponses.
The underlying reasoning and evidence
remain fragmented, assumptions can escape scrutiny, and criticism loses its target as debate
moves on.
Consequently, incompatible worldviews can persist without their disagreements be-
ing resolved. This talk proposes a new politics, motivated by Active Inference, which provides
the mathematical object around which political debate can be organized: the policy map. This
map expresses a worldview through predicted outcomes under alternative policies.
Making it
the persistent object of debate allows criticism to retain a stable address rather than disappear
in the rhetoric of successive exchanges. The mathematics of the policy map distinguishes three
types of criticism: belief criticism, challenging beliefs about current states or the consequences of
policies; outcome incompleteness, identifying omitted consequences; and policy incompleteness,
identifying missing alternatives. These provide the horizontal sorting of political debate. Vertical
sorting organizes the reasoning beneath each prediction, allowing criticism to reach its underlying
assumptions and evidence. Unresolved objections remain visible until answered or incorporated
through revision, potentially changing the predicted outcomes and preferred policies. In this way,
Active Inference offers a normative application that goes beyond describing politics as if political
actors performed Bayesian inference. It motivates transforming the very structure of political de-
bate, replacing rhetorical exchanges with cumulative criticism and incentivizing a shared search
for the worldview that best fits the available evidence. The ultimate aim is a politics in which
criticism can actually bite, transforming not only political debates, but the world shaped by its
decisions. For the only path forward is critique without exception.

Presenter: Montijn van Loo
Contribution: Livestreamed panel or group session
Affiliations: Tilburg University
ORCID: 0009-0003-0249-3240
Presenter background
I am an econometrist gone rogue. Instead of throwing mathematics at finance, I throw Active
Inference mathematics at philosophy. See for the consciousness, morality, politics, gender, free

Abstract
Consciousness: The Channel Framework and its Application to INTREPID The Channel Framework
treats theories of consciousness as relating physical and experiential events, with the minimal re-
lation being predictive. The Channel Framework embeds this psychophysical relation within the
full experimental prediction architecture, connecting physical processes, conscious content, intro-
spective access, and observable outcomes. Expressed through probabilistic channels, these archi-
tectures can be interpreted as generative models of experimental predictions, providing a common
language in which different theories can be explicated and compared. I apply the framework to
INTREPID’s adversarial comparison of Integrated Information Theory and predictive-processing
theories of consciousness, including Neurorepresentationalism and the Active Inference theory
of consciousness (AI-C). The framework asks whether their prediction architectures can be faith-
fully aligned, which theoretical differences actually generate their contrasting experimental pre-
dictions, and whether these differences carry the particular claims the experiments aim to test.
Building on this analysis, the Channel Standard develops criteria for attributing evidence from
competing theory predictions to specific theoretical claims. The broader aim is to move beyond
theory-level comparison toward methodological concensus.

An elementary form of agency at planetary-scale
Presenter: Sergio Rubin
Contribution: Livestreamed panel or group session
Abstract
The claim that climate homeorhesis—non-equilibrium steady-state dynamics that underlie Earth’s
long-term habitability—depends on an integrated biosphere–climate system capable of acting on
its own is central to debates surrounding the Gaia hypothesis.
This paper presents a proof of
concept suggesting a minimal form of planetary agency based on a simple biosphere–climate sys-
tem model operating through active inference. This means that the internal states of the system
(biosphere) will appear to infer shifts in net incoming solar radiation (external states) through
its boundary states, i.e. by integrating past and current surface temperatures (sensory states)
and actively changing greenhouse forcing (active states) to maintain climate trajectories within
geophysiological bounds. When surface temperature is altered by solar fluctuation changes, the
system adjusts greenhouse and albedo parameters to restore expected climate trajectories, anal-
ogous to correcting sensory prediction errors in biological systems. Perturbing active states (e.g.,
imposed changes in albedo) does not reduce inference precision. Inference robustness decreases
when the system’s degrees of freedom increase. Active inference falls, and thus climate home-
orhesis fails when there is no biosphere activity (lifeless planet) or when it is decoupled from the
climate system. Predictive behaviour operates within the range of solar constants consistent with
life’s presence on Earth (~ 4 Ga to ~ 1.5 Ga expected in the future); outside this range, inference
fails, undermining a putative form of planetary agency.

Abstract
In multi-agent social settings, model reliability varies across relationships. Beyond inferring what
others will do, an agent must calibrate how confidently those inferences should guide policy se-

An agent may maintain a well-validated model of one partner, a
fragile model of another, and a model under revision for a third; collapsing these into a single con-

confidence accumulation and revision depend on model parameters. Together, these results show
how relationship-specific affective precision can distinguish social prediction from social policy
commitment.

Abstract
Active Inference commonly assumes a fixed, pre-specified action space and sufficiently informa-

mechanism for incorporating and adapting newly introduced actions in Active Inference, allowing
their transition models to become progressively better aligned with the underlying environment
