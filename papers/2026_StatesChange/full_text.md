Abstract
How can a federation sustain several nations without claiming the whole persons it governs? This article develops composition
without capture through Blake’s distinction between enduring persons and changing states, his accounts of national life, and the
coercion that can accompany creative system-making. Close readings of Milton, Jerusalem and the Last Judgment prose ask
who can create, interpret, contest and revise a common form. The institutional construction distinguishes aﬀiliation, poetic
interpretation and competence: shared histories and state readings do not themselves authorize an institution to impose a
particular decision. A formal countermodel makes that gap explicit. Five hypothetical Xeno-California conflicts then test ten
alternatives against fifteen stipulated safeguards. Their 40,960 intervention selections yield 31 distinct within-case records,
exposing the difference between selecting a repair and changing a decision. Person-specific comparisons distinguish nested
requirements from incomparable tradeoffs. An atlas of 21,454 timing configurations separates feasible filing, uncovered waiting
and two consequence duties; eight pressure cases retain material support, reprisal, reviewer control and disputed jurisdiction
beyond those interval calculations. These are constructed analyses, not observations of functioning institutions or a political
program attributed to Blake. A separate corpus inquiry compares specified word forms across 199 primary extracts and 33
recorded source families, keeping copy repetition, weighting and source deletion visible. The resulting account ties shared rule
to continuing provision and effective powers of challenge. A common world remains contestable when people can dispute both
an act and its account of who they are while retaining the means to live and participate.
1 Common institutions, plural nations and persons who exceed their roles
A shared language needs teachers, meeting places and decisions about resources. None of these needs settles who may speak
for its speakers, whether one organization should receive every grant, or what happens when a member disputes its account of
the tradition. The problem begins inside collective life: the institutions that sustain it can also monopolize its interpretation.
Composition without capture names the task of making common institutions while preserving participants’ power to challenge
their terms. Blake brings this problem into view through makers whose creative work both shelters persons and subjects them
to a system.
1.1 Creating a system; releasing people from its authority
Los’s declaration in Jerusalem makes collective order a problem of making: who creates its form, whose labor sustains it, and
who can alter what it demands?
I must Create a System, or be enslav'd by another Mans
I will not Reason & Compare: my business is to Create
Q30. William Blake, Jerusalem 10:20–21, E153. ( Blake, n.d.b).
He is building Golgonooza through labor, conflict and the transformation of suffering. The declaration opposes enslavement,
yet the surrounding scene refuses an easy identification of creation with freedom. Immediately before it, Los compels his
Spectre to work; afterward he demands obedience and threatens greater torment. The Spectre invokes a sacrificial law against
the children Los seeks to protect. As Los’s divided power, the Spectre also makes this a struggle over the maker’s pride and
self-righteousness. Creation and domination occupy the same workshop [ Blake (n.d.b), 8:21–40, E151; 10:17–40, E153].
The critical disagreement concerns what Los is doing when he creates. Stephen Clark’s review of Birenbaum asks whether the
emphasis falls on system or creation, but also challenges the procedure that converts a dramatic utterance into a doctrine of
unrestricted creative freedom. Attention to speakers, narrative and historical setting can change the meaning of the declaration
itself ( Clark, 1995, pp. 69–70). White makes a more specific and less reassuring proposal: Los’s refusal to reason and compare
rejects induction in favor of a priori rational construction. On that reading, creation may produce a coherent abstraction
rather than knowledge responsive to experienced particulars ( White, 2005, pp. 116–117). These positions cannot simply be
combined into a celebration of openness. They make the next declaration decisive for this article: whatever kind of creation
Los undertakes, its political significance must be assessed alongside his work of releasing individuals from systems. The maker’s
independence does not establish the freedom of those who live within the work.

1.2 National arts distribute powers to make, select and revise
Blake’s public address in Jerusalem describes the craft of a common form before the poem’s visionary institutions unfold:

Q33. William Blake, Jerusalem 3, prose, E146. ( Blake, n.d.b).
The differences concern cadence, syllable and tone. Their mutual necessity does not erase the hierarchy between terrific, gentle
and inferior parts, or tell us who may dispute their placement. Viscomi adds a material complication: in illuminated printing,
execution can generate invention. Blake makes decisions on the copper rather than simply transferring an already completed
page design; further printing can become an occasion for further invention. Viscomi also explicitly rejects the inference that
Blake originally invented the technique to overthrow conventional publishing ( Viscomi, 2012, sec. IV, paragraphs beginning
“With no page designs” and “Illuminated printing evolved”). The political extension therefore needs its own argument. An
institution whose members only execute an authorized design distributes creative power differently from one in which their
experience can change the design. Inclusion as a necessary part is insuﬀicient if the right to originate or revise the whole remains
elsewhere.
Blake immediately gives artistic organization national stakes:

are Destroy'd or Flourish! The Primeval State of Man, was Wisdom,
Art, and Science.

which makers become visible and which account of a nation’s achievements acquires authority. Stefanis shows this process at
work in Blake’s 1809 exhibition. Blake selected his painterly work while omitting engravings, presenting a career suited to

1.3 Enduring persons, changing states and the value of national histories
The central distinction appears in Milton’s instruction on combination:
Distinguish therefore States from Individuals in those States.
States Change: but Individual Identities never change nor cease:
You cannot go to Eternal Death in that which can never Die.
Q03. William Blake, Milton 32:22-24, E132. ( Blake, n.d.e).
The Seven Angels distinguish combination in freedom and brotherhood from combination through war, sacrifice and impris-
onment. The issue is how a multitude is composed, rather than whether it is a multitude. Individual identities also endure;
the contrast is not an eternal collective against expendable persons. Imagination itself is not a state ( Blake, n.d.e, 32:8–38,
E131–132). An oﬀicial therefore cannot acquire a person’s identity by classifying the state through which that person passes.
A federation sustains relations among imaginative possibilities and the historical national formations through which they
become articulate. A nation may disclose several possibilities; a possibility may recur across several national histories. Their
relation is many-to-many. This is a constructive distinction made through Blake, whose Last Judgment prose also describes
states appearing as multitudes of nations. It must answer to that visionary use rather than pretend to supply an exhaustive
dictionary of his terms ( Blake, n.d.c, notebook p. 76, second passage, E556–557). Damrosch’s account of universality in
particular manifestations reinforces the need to resist a detachable essence that an institution could certify as a people’s real
nature ( Damrosch, 1980, pp. 15–21, especially p. 20 n. 25).
Temporal production has aﬀirmative value:
Eternity is in love with the productions of time.
Q13. William Blake, The Marriage of Heaven and Hell, plate 7, E36. ( Blake, n.d.d).
A language, friendship or public achievement need not last forever to matter. Its place among the Proverbs of Hell also prevents
the line from becoming a freestanding approval of everything history produces. A shared school and a punitive register are
both productions of time; they call for different judgments. The enduring availability of a state supplies no mandate to rule:
Jerusalem makes the state Satan permanent and accursed so that Albion may rise from it ( Blake, n.d.b, 31:13–16, E177–178).
Intelligibility, value and authority require separate arguments.
Fallon gives national attachment a positive role: a situated homeland can sustain imaginative cultivation and relations with
other nations. His distinction between Jerusalem’s growth and the Polypus’s coercive aggregation also answers a particular
rival reading. As Fallon reports it, Wright finds structural similarity between the bonds of love and the Polypus; Fallon argues
that the poems distinguish their forms of growth even while making them dangerously confusable ( Fallon, 2015, submitted ms.
pp. 10–12, especially p. 12 n. 40). This disagreement matters because an institution may describe assimilation as loving union.
Recognition of several nations does not settle the objection if a single center still decides which forms of national life count
as properly fulfilled. The proposal here values common histories while requiring their institutional interpreters to answer to
participants who can dissent, remain differently aﬀiliated, or propose another account of the common work.
Mellor’s objection concerns the agency distributed within the shared form. Inclusive fraternity and androgyny can preserve a
division in which masculine creation directs feminine embodiment; Ostriker’s partly inconsistent orientations prevent a single
story of emancipation from settling the issue ( Mellor, 1982/1983, pp. 148–153; Ostriker, 1982/1983, pp. 156–164). Participation
must include powers to originate, revise and refuse a form, alongside a place within it. The nation that includes everyone
symbolically can still reserve those powers to a few.

Composition without capture names the political commitment developed here. Languages, memories, solidarities and shared
achievements can sustain collective life without giving an organization an exhaustive claim over its participants. Nor does non-

2.5 Distance changes how a person, state and nation appear
A Vision of the Last Judgment unsettles a strict lexical division between eternal states and temporal nations. Blake first
distinguishes Moses and Abraham as persons from the states signified by their names. He then describes those states appearing
as one man at a distance and as multitudes of nations when approached. Nations participate here in visionary representation,
while the apparent individual changes with the observer’s distance. The political reconstruction therefore distinguishes historical
national formations from persons without treating those categories as Blake’s exhaustive taxonomy ( Blake, n.d.c, notebook p. 76,
second passage, E556–557).

2.6 Endurance grants no mandate to rule
The eternity of Rahab or the availability of a state named Satan supplies a decisive limit on the reconstruction. A vailability
or intelligibility does not make a state desirable. A federation that contains states might preserve the ability to recognize,
encounter and pass through a harmful condition without authorizing its domination. Giving every state a sovereign seat would
confuse a vocabulary for understanding experience with a rule for allocating coercion.
The Platonic hypothesis remains open. An essence model makes historical nations instances of stable forms; a possibility model
treats states as recurrent relational possibilities; a dramatic model retains their place in a visionary narrative; a regulative
model uses them to orient inquiry without constitutionalizing metaphysics. The present draft favors investigating possibility
and drama while requiring publicly contestable institutional reasons. The readings below compare what each account explains
about permanence and particularity.
The dispute turns on the kind of permanence at stake. The publisher summary of Larrissy’s chapter frames Blake’s Neoplatonic
inheritance as a question of extent and character ( Larrissy, 1994). Damrosch locates universality in particular manifestations
and in Jesus, resisting detachable forms; his Cusanus comparison is distinguished from a claim of demonstrated influence
(Damrosch, 1980, pp. 15–21 and p. 20 n. 25). The reconstruction accordingly treats recurrence as a relation among particular
enactments rather than a national essence available for oﬀicial certification.
Lee’s introduction complicates the equation of plasticity with emancipation: pre-existent form can protect identity against
demands for indefinitely remaking life ( Lee, 2026, pp. 1–10). A continually changing collective can force people to adapt to
an authorized future, while a persistent claim about personhood may protect them against it. The political question concerns
who invokes permanence or becoming, and with what consequences.

3 National generation, reciprocal exchange and the struggle over agency
3.1 National generation is embodied work, inheritance and the making of new publics
Nation as the born or generations is the proposed conceptual extension, not an invented Blake quotation. Biological ancestry,
historical founding, artistic production, migration, initiation into a practice and the emergence of a new public are different
senses of generation. The theory cannot slide between them to make descent a citizenship test. A person not born locally must
retain an arguable route into political membership; a person born within a recognized nation must retain the ability to dissent
from its institutions.
Inclosing all the Children of Los time after time.
Their Giant forms condensing into Nations & Peoples & Tongues
Q24. William Blake, Jerusalem 53:7–8, E202. ( Blake, n.d.b).
Los repeatedly encloses his children before their forms condense into nations, peoples and tongues. The account moves through
organs, furnaces and a spiritual London continually built and decaying ( Blake, n.d.b, 53:1–29, E202–203). National generation
is organized work with pain and confinement as well as creative possibility. A language persists through speakers, teachers,
carers, publishers and places to meet. Recognition can support that work while concentrating every grant in one certified body.
Supporting a practice and certifying its exclusive representative are different decisions. Younger participants can challenge a
school or cultural budget without first proving that they constitute another nation; newcomers can change a tradition through
their contribution to its continuation.

3.2 Particularity and differentiated provision: relevant differences under common standing
Heppner complicates an opposition between particularity and visual simplification. His reading of The Woman Taken in
Adultery finds sparse setting and restrained expression while locating significance in posture, narrative and words beyond the
image. In the dispute with Reynolds, the issue is discrimination of character rather than accumulation of incidental detail
(Heppner, 1983, pp. 55–56, 59). This changes the standard for a classification record. A longer identity file can overlook who
controls a claimant’s wages; a limited record can protect a claim whose grounds require no complete personal narrative. The
record needs the difference relevant to the decision and a way for the affected person to name what it misses.
The final maxim of The Marriage of Heaven and Hell makes the consequences of a common rule depend on the beings it
governs:
One Law for the Lion & Ox is Oppression
Q37. William Blake, The Marriage of Heaven and Hell, plate 24, E44. ( Blake, n.d.d).
Identical requirements can impose unequal burdens. A hearing during paid working hours, a fee indifferent to means or a water
charge indifferent to access makes the distribution of time and resources part of its operation. Equal standing therefore calls
for an account of relevant differences and differentiated provision. The lion and ox supply no natural ranks for a governing
body to certify: its classification and the resulting treatment both need reasons and a challenge route. The surrounding Fancy
also exposes the interpreter’s power. After the Angel’s conversion, the narrator announces a Bible of Hell that the world will
receive whether it wants it or not. A defense of difference can accompany an insistence on interpretive command ( Blake, n.d.d,
plates 22–24, E43–44). Frosch similarly objects to defending Urizen’s One Law through its declared intentions of peace and
forgiveness: the speech’s satirical structure matters to the judgment ( Frosch, 1979, p. 44).

National mediation and national possession are different relations. Fallon interprets the homeland as a setting for imaginative

federation’s self-description need not mark a failure to mature. The constitutional question is whether it can dispute that
description while retaining voice, material security and an arguable claim to powers of its own.
Matthews historicizes the vocabulary of sex and sexuality and challenges the assumption that late Blake simply rejects erotic

every one to accept its final metaphysical description of humanity. The non-exhaustion rule must therefore constrain the federa-
tion’s interpretive authority: people need standing to challenge the terms of recognition and the classifier’s competence without
first endorsing the poetic-state vocabulary. Historical creativity cannot warrant treating a people’s refusal as imaginative
immaturity. This places the burden of justification on the federation as well as its constituent bodies.
3.4 Exchange makes national particularity relational
The closing stanza of To the Jews names a mutual action rather than the merger of lands into one national subject:
In my Exchanges every Land
Shall walk, & mine in every Land,
Mutual shall build Jerusalem:
Both heart in heart & hand in hand.
Q14. William Blake, Jerusalem 27:85–88, E173. ( Blake, n.d.b).
The grammar joins distinct agents through exchange: each land walks in the other’s exchanges, and both build. The reciprocal
terms support a relation among national productions rather than a single owner of the universal. Yet the prose address
framing this stanza makes religious claims about Jews and the Bible. Reciprocity in the verse cannot erase the conversionary
and supersessionist pressure a reader may find in that address. A federation that adopts the exchange image has to defend
participation without demanding a common redemptive history. Fallon’s account of a situated national opening and the
competing incorporation reading therefore confront the same material page ( Fallon, 2015, submitted ms. pp. 4, 10–12; Blake,
n.d.b, plate 27, E171–174).
The exchange image changes the institutional question from how many cultures are recognized to who controls the exchange. A
language service can widen participation while a compulsory identity register narrows it. The proposed decision record therefore
distinguishes the provider, recipient, assigned role and public consequence. The parties need power to negotiate terms, refuse
an offered classification and contest the institution that certifies their common good.
Kaplan connects this address to the poem’s criticism of patriarchal possession: a family elevated over the rest of the world
can become an instrument of destruction ( Kaplan, 1996, pp. 68–69). This gives the national argument an internal rather than
merely external test. A nation can participate in exchange while retaining an exclusive right to decide which members embody
its authentic history. Conversely, a federation can condemn national self-enclosure while absorbing every people’s account of
its origins into its own story. The proposed reconstruction must refuse both privileges. A people should be able to dispute a
common narrative without thereby surrendering its voice in shared affairs; its oﬀicers should face challenges from those whom
its own narrative misrecognizes.
Heppner’s reading of the early drawing catalogued as Butlin 92 makes the woman-city an agent rather than simply a destination.
He proposes reading a supposed number as Gog’s name and understands the descending Jerusalem as driving him away. His
comparison with biblical narratives and earlier designs makes compositional transformation consequential: the image can
generate a prophetic relation rather than merely reproduce an antecedent text ( Heppner, 1986, pp. 4–5, 9–10). This reading
prevents us from treating every female personification as passive by definition. It also opens a harder question for the political
reconstruction: whose agency is represented when a nation appears as a woman? The force of a collective personification cannot
stand in for the powers of the women, workers or dissenters associated with it.

3.5 The public image of a nation distributes credibility
Matthews reads the 1809 exhibition as an intervention in national visual culture. Her argument connects public art, Barry and

A school history, civic monument or association’s celebration can distribute credibility before its organizers exercise formal
jurisdiction. Some participants appear as makers of the common world; others appear as its moral problem. Non-exhaustion
therefore concerns the means of representation as well as an oﬀicer’s disclaimer that a body speaks only for a purpose. Par-

Jerusalem 3’s arts statement makes this question internal to national flourishing: cultural provision can widen production while

Blake also supplies a countertext to the preferred non-fusion thesis. His letter to Flaxman of 19 October 1801 hopes that France

to defend is between making a common artistic world and authorizing a common government: neither undertaking fixes the
rightful scale or powers of the other.
3.6 A shared national voice can carry incompatible political projects
Ferber traces the hymn commonly called Jerusalem through wartime mobilization and women’s suffrage, and follows how
performance and editorial changes alter its address. First-person vows become a collective undertaking through a chorus
(Ferber, 2000, pp. 83–86, 87–89). A national work is made again through its use: common words and melody can sustain
different political projects. The reception history makes national production concrete without assigning the later users’ purposes
to Blake.
The distinction has an institutional consequence. A representative body may commission a shared performance and invite
several communities. Attending, contributing, endorsing a statement, authorizing a representative and consenting to a decision
remain separate acts. Singing need not surrender a treaty claim; declining to sing need not reject common provision. A record
that collapses these acts can manufacture authorization from evidence of contact. Participants can care deeply about the
common work while disputing who invokes it in their name.

3.7 Gender and the supposedly universal subject
Mellor distinguishes imaginative reciprocity from sexual equality. Brotherhood and androgyny can leave creative labor and
authority unequally distributed; mutual dependence can coexist with subordination ( Mellor, 1982/1983, pp. 148–153). Her
reading of Jerusalem plate 99 challenges the apparent symmetry of the final transformation. The question for this reconstruction
is therefore concrete: who can initiate a claim, interpret the common vocabulary, refuse a function and redistribute authority?
Kaplan’s central distinction sharpens the manuscript’s gender objection. An attack on possessive patriarchs need not produce
equal authority between genders. A brotherhood can redistribute power among men while leaving women responsible for the
relations through which that fraternity becomes possible ( Kaplan, 1996, pp. 70–71, 77–78). The relevant rival interpretation
therefore accepts much of the emancipatory reading before challenging its conclusion. Deliverance from jealous ownership,
criticism of punitive morality and aﬀirmation of sexual life may coexist with a hierarchy of creative subjects and emanated
participants. Listing those emancipatory commitments does not answer the objection. The reconstruction has to explain which
relations it can inherit and which it must transform.
The diﬀiculty reaches the subject of our reconstruction. A grammar may insist that persons exceed classifications while reserving
interpretation and decision for a privileged participant and assigning mediation, care or embodiment to others. Its universal
person can therefore be partial before any classification begins. The test asks who may initiate a claim, refuse a function,
interpret the shared vocabulary and redistribute authority. Neutral names for the sorts in Definition 1 do not themselves
remove a hierarchy built into their relations.
Ostriker identifies four partly inconsistent orientations: liberation, complementarity, erotic confinement and female subordi-
nation. Oothoon’s agency does not secure deliverance, and the late poems leave the distribution of mediation and authority
contested ( Ostriker, 1982/1983, pp. 156–164). These orientations cut across any simple story of an early emancipatory Blake
followed by a uniformly patriarchal one. They direct attention to relationships within the proposed union, including the position
of the person who refuses its promised harmony.
Freedom to refuse must include the possibility of remaining. Mellor’s criticism of gendered creative priority asks who determines
the purposes of a shared world, even when everyone is said to contribute to it ( Mellor, 1982/1983, pp. 149–153). Simpson’s
account of a person who aﬀirms citizenship while contesting exclusion from membership puts a related problem in a different
political setting ( Simpson, 2007, pp. 76–78). A person may reject an assigned role without wishing to abandon a people, place
or relationship. Supported passage therefore needs a companion: standing to challenge the terms of participation from within.
The federation must protect that challenge without requiring an exhaustive account of the claimant’s identity or assuming that
it originates the people’s authority.

4 From imaginative federation to shared rule and self-rule
Architecture A is a federation of enduring relational possibilities. Historical nations articulate, combine and transform those
possibilities through particular practices. The federal whole is enacted in translation, encounter, common work, hospitality,
disagreement and passage. It is not exclusively embodied by a dominant nation. National formations contribute particular
histories and practices without acquiring ownership of the possibilities they disclose or of the people who sustain them.
Architecture A specifies a relation among possibilities, historical formations and persons. It preserves intelligibility and en-
counter across changing aﬀiliations. Architecture B supplies the institutional questions this relation opens: shared rule and
self-rule, public revenue, adjudication and representation. Its criterion is the distribution of justified powers and continuing
duties, including the power to challenge how the common world has been described.
Architecture B distinguishes that imaginative constitution from a legal constitution. The latter must allocate real powers
among territorial units, task-specific authorities, national-cultural bodies and shared institutions. A watershed and a language
community may need different boundaries. A migrant may be subject to a jurisdiction without belonging to a recognized
national association. A nation may cross jurisdictions without acquiring an unlimited police power over its members.
The institutional construction starts from equal personhood, bodily security, conscience, expression, due process, material
access and contestability. These are its normative premises. Differentiated arrangements can protect equal standing where
uniform administration reproduces dispossession. Each asymmetry therefore needs a stated purpose, an affected population,
an account of its burdens and a means of challenge.
Three proposed mechanisms translate the grammar into institutional questions. A translation obligation requires accessible
reasons across national narratives and an account of whom the deciding institution does not represent. A passage guarantee
protects portable basic standing and practical aﬀiliation change without eliminating legitimate continuing debts or duties. A
non-exhaustion rule denies an organization’s claim to speak every member’s whole identity. Together, these mechanisms specify
the institutional work of composition without capture.

4.3 Counterpublics and the means of contestation
Fraser makes the right to remain collective as well as individual. Her counterpublics provide settings in which subordinated
people develop interpretations of their needs and identities; their withdrawal and regrouping can support intervention in wider
public debate. She also warns that counterpublics can reproduce exclusion ( Fraser, 1992, pp. 122–124). A federation should
therefore protect the formation of opposition within and across recognized nations without equating every opposition with a
new sovereign unit. A women’s forum, a workers’ publication or an unaﬀiliated residents’ assembly may need resources and
access to common institutions while refusing the claim to represent an entire people. Cultural plurality cannot be measured
only by the organizations already awarded seats.

Her argument about participation also exceeds the removal of formal barriers: unequal social relations can continue to structure
discussion after a forum admits everyone ( Fraser, 1992, pp. 118–121). In this reconstruction, reimbursement for a hearing
cannot be assumed to neutralize dependence on an employer or an association that controls housing. Support must reach the
conditions under which people prepare, speak and return to daily life. A study should therefore examine who can lose work,
care or accommodation through contestation, alongside who appears on a participant list.
This changes what the translation obligation must secure. A claimant needs more than permission to submit reasons in an
authorized language: people need places and time to formulate reasons together before presenting them to a body they oppose.
Funding only oﬀicial national representatives can make dissent dependent on the very institutions it challenges. A possible
arrangement would support independent meeting space, interpretation, childcare and circulation of arguments, with allocation
criteria that opponents can themselves question. These are proposals for the present reconstruction. They do not follow from
labeling a group a counterpublic, and their effect would depend on whether participants actually control the relevant means.
Fraser distinguishes publics that form opinion from those that also make decisions, then raises the accountability of decision-
making bodies to people affected outside them ( Fraser, 1992, pp. 134–136). That distinction exposes a weakness in a record
that simply names a consultation forum. A resident may be fully heard while every consequential power remains elsewhere. The
record must identify what can change after an intervention: the agenda, the evidence considered, an allocation, an enforcement
condition or the rule of representation. Different bodies can carry different powers, but the connection between argument and
decision must be stated. Independence from oﬀicials and influence over oﬀicials are separate institutional achievements.
This also prevents counterpublics from becoming another compulsory national taxonomy. People may organize around an injury
that crosses languages and histories, or around an exclusion inside a recognized community. They need not prove a distinct
collective essence to secure a hearing. The federation’s obligation would concern access and power for the specified dispute, not
the certification of an additional permanent identity. A shared world remains open when its participants can create new sites
of argument without obtaining prior approval for their account of who they are.

5.3 Initiative, independence and revision are different powers
Shachar’s arbitration example makes the burden of initiating review explicit. Mandatory scrutiny before an award becomes
enforceable removes the requirement that a vulnerable party start the protective process ( Shachar, 2005, p. 76 and nn. 89–90).
This is a different mechanism from merely telling the person where to complain. The comparison below distinguishes automatic
protection from protection triggered by a feasible filing. Both name a provider and an interval. Their consequences differ when
the threatened deprivation begins before the filing window opens.
An oﬀice’s separate identity also records less than independence. Pettit assumes independent and impartial appeals ( Pettit,
2005, p. 9). Distinct oﬀice identifiers do not establish independent appointments, budgets or evidence access. The audit calls
this predicate issuer separation . It records a relationship that can be checked without treating a different name as evidence
of impartiality. Similarly, suspension, advice and revision do different work. A forum that can suspend an act can interrupt its
operation; a forum that can revise can change it. Shachar’s power to overrule an inequitable aspect of an agreement supplies
a concrete comparator for consequential review ( Shachar, 2005, p. 77). A claimant need not win for review to matter, but an
advisory recommendation and a binding revision do not confer the same power.
The disagreement about procedures goes deeper. Pettit’s account presupposes admissible reasons through which decisions
can be challenged; Tully opens the procedures and criteria of reasonable argument themselves to contestation ( Pettit, 2005,
pp. 8–9; Tully, 2006, pp. 25–27). A forum might hear objections to a water allocation while refusing objections to the categories
defining whose interests count. Revising that allocation, revising the classification rule and contesting the forum’s competence
are distinct questions. Tully’s separation of dialogue from decision keeps other institutional routes available when negotiation
leaves minorities exposed ( Tully, 2006, p. 29). Individual admission in the present audit supplies no mandate to determine a
people’s collective or treaty claim.

5.6 Challenging a decision without reproducing its dependency
What must remain secure while a person tries to use the hearing? H12 deliberately left this question open. A completed filing

calculation; others concern powers or relations that the seven-condition profile does not represent. Keeping the distinction
visible allows the audit to identify its own limit without treating every unknown as a demonstrated failure.

6 Plural peoples, overlapping powers and the conditions of non-domination
Nicolaïdis’s demoicracy is the closest concise comparator: plural peoples acting jointly without fusion into one demos. Her
normative benchmark is not a description of every European practice, and an extension to overlapping intra-state nations
requires new argument. Equal standing among peoples and equal influence among persons can conflict. The project must
defend voting rules rather than promise that all forms of equality coincide. ( Nicolaïdis, 2013).
Nicolaïdis distinguishes a normative benchmark from observed European practice, develops recognition among demoi, and
addresses voting, rights, mediation and accountability ( Nicolaïdis, 2013, author ms. pp. 7–16). Joint rule concerns the terms
on which people remain capable of governing together. Mutual recognition must therefore confront the control of dissenters
within each constituent people as well as relations among its governing bodies.
Kymlicka’s author synopsis differentiates minority-rights claims, while Shachar’s joint-governance article examines authority
sharing and vulnerable insiders ( Kymlicka, 1995, author synopsis; Shachar, 2005, pp. 71–77). Together they prevent recognition
of a nation from becoming an undifferentiated entitlement to govern everyone classified within it. Exit cannot substitute for
voice when departure costs housing, livelihood, language access or family relations. ( Kymlicka, 1995); ( Shachar, 2001).
Bauböck supplies a direct challenge to national-cultural autonomy based on politically consequential registers. A nominal
choice among permitted identities may still impose exclusive identification, exclude mixed or new aﬀiliations, and entangle
franchise or taxation. Open cultural association and public jurisdiction must remain different relations. This objection cannot
be answered by renaming an exclusive register fluid. ( Bauböck, 2001).
Bauböck distinguishes cultural liberty, recognition and political autonomy, then examines consequential registers and the
exclusion of multiple identification ( Bauböck, 2001, PDF pp. 3–7, 21–26). A many-to-many cultural map leaves the franchise
unsettled. A full public vote for every aﬀiliation may duplicate influence; one compulsory final aﬀiliation restores exclusivity
at the point of decision. The response has to distinguish equal general representation from several limited memberships and
defend its fiscal effects and provision for unrecognized people.
Ostrom’s polycentric governance asks about rules and performance; Hooghe and Marks distinguish general-purpose and task-
specific architectures. Neither turns a cultural nation into a functional jurisdiction. Overlap can enable adaptation or diffuse
responsibility. Water, labor, housing and revenue may resist tidy decomposition. Their historical California illustrations are
not current evaluations of California institutions. ( Ostrom, 2010); ( Hooghe and Marks , 2003).
Ostrom treats property as a bundle of rights and develops design principles as contextual regularities rather than universal
rules ( Ostrom, 2010, pp. 651–653). This shifts the comparison from the number of governing bodies to the rules that organize
entry, information, decisions, benefits and sanctions. A watershed authority must be assessed through those relations and the
resource it governs.
Hooghe and Marks distinguish general-purpose Type I from task-specific Type II arrangements. Nonintersecting Type I
memberships can coexist with overlapping competencies; Type II bodies often operate within Type I settings and have variable
franchises ( Hooghe and Marks , 2003, pp. 233–241, especially p. 237 n. 6). Stable territorial citizenship can therefore coexist
with shared water competence, while a functional body can still exclude affected residents from its electorate. Boundary overlap
and democratic inclusion are separate questions.
Treaty federalism raises a prior question: a people may contest the federation’s authority to incorporate it. This concerns the
source of jurisdiction, rather than simply the range of cultural choices available within an accepted state. It changes what
counts as agreement to the common institutions ( Henderson, 2019; Simpson, 2007).
Henderson treats treaty federalism as constitutive of Canadian authority, rather than an added minority concession. Inherent
powers and continuing treaty relationships lead into a territorial account of treaty governance and proposals for structural
and fiscal change ( Henderson, 2019, pp. 21–41). His institutional argument contains a consequential tension: direct treaty
representation could lessen reliance on consent, consultation and accommodation (p. 38), while consolidation requires each
Treaty Nation’s legitimate consent (p. 40). Representation and agreement therefore have to be distinguished within the design
itself.
A federation cannot prove its title by first classifying every people as one of its constituents. Authority to recognize and
allocate powers may itself be contested. The proposed record therefore needs a jurisdictional remainder : a question not

presumed resolved when other fields are completed. This is our term. A people may negotiate without conceding that the
forum originates its standing. Aﬀiliation, residence, property claims, public competence and treaty relations remain separately
describable, but decomposition does not make them materially independent. Dividing a land relationship into a language
service and a water entitlement may discard the very claim under dispute.
A delegate’s presence, a mandate to negotiate, assent to a particular agreement and a continuing power to reopen it also differ.
An ordinary shared decision cannot promise every person’s agreement, yet a general majority cannot be assumed to dispose of

The UN Declaration connects self-determination and institutions (Articles 3–5), participation and consultation toward consent
(18–19), land and redress (25–28), projects and institutions (32–35), and cross-border relations and treaties (36–37). Its sex-

Pettit’s account makes non-capture more demanding than freedom from actual punishment. Dependency persists when someone
controls access to housing, work or essential services, even if that power is exercised benevolently. Protection from private power
and control of the protective state are separate requirements ( Pettit, 2012, pp. 3–10). A new appeals authority must therefore
be assessed for the power it acquires as well as the protection it promises.
Pettit also distinguishes collective electoral authorization from the control retained by persons in their plurality. Reactive,
representative and regulative contestation require public reasons and effective correction; representatives can nevertheless
become lobbies, and appeals depend on impartiality ( Pettit, 2005, pp. 1–4, 8–14). Voice, practical exit and revision impose
different demands: a forum must hear challenges, a person needs means to leave, and rules must be open to change.
The relevant capacity is the power to make a challenge change what an authority may do. Pettit’s account includes procedures
that constrain decisions before a person must appeal ( Pettit, 2005, pp. 9–14). Shachar makes this concrete by proposing review
as a condition of enforceability in family-law arbitration, with an agreed standard and an identified reviewing body ( Shachar,
2005, pp. 75–77 and p. 76 nn. 89–90). Applied to the hypothetical watershed, the comparison specifies who initiates scrutiny,
who pays, what happens to essential provision during delay and whether relief can alter the allocation. A procedure that
requires workers to risk their wages before it operates may preserve the dependency it promises to remedy.
The contribution is a critical grammar joining nonidentity, national generation and imaginative passage to specific institutional
transitions. Demoicracy supplies joint rule among plural peoples; autonomy and non-domination supply tests of power. Blake
supplies a demanding account of the person who exceeds a present state. The proposed record asks where those accounts agree,
where their demands conflict and what decision follows.
6.1 An institutional comparator: two chambers, resources and remedies
The Swiss constitutional text examined here makes several equalities coexist through different institutions. Population-based
representation in the National Council operates alongside a separate cantonal representation rule in the Council of States;
federal parliamentary decisions require both chambers’ agreement. Political rights cannot be exercised contemporaneously in
several Cantons. Financial autonomy has its own provisions for suﬀicient resources and equalization ( Swiss Confederation ,
2024, arts. 39(3), 47(2), 135, 149–150, 156(2)). This historical English text, consolidated to 1 January 2024, identifies choices
for the proposed federation: which units receive collective representation, how population affects influence, and how poorer
participants obtain means to govern.
Collective voice differs from a person’s access to protection. The text ties the federal political franchise to Swiss citizenship
and eligibility while providing procedural guarantees to every person, subject to specified conditions and exceptions ( Swiss
Confederation, 2024, arts. 29–29a, 136). Participation in an allocation hearing, access to legal assistance, a vote and a
collective jurisdictional claim do different work. In the drought case, the federation must say which powers reach a migrant
worker and what support makes them usable. Cantonal seats supply no answer to that question, and the court’s jurisdiction
and applicable-law provisions delimit the available remedy ( Swiss Confederation , 2024, arts. 189–190).
The comparison separates territorial constituent units from cultural nations. It also exposes a substantive limit on treating
any federation as a pluralist exemplar: Article 72(3) prohibits minarets ( Swiss Confederation , 2024, art. 72(3)). Institutional
diversity has to be examined alongside specific exclusions. Treaty relations pose the further question of the federation’s title to
decide, which a cantonal analogy does not settle.
6.2 Revising the procedures of recognition
Tully places the governing procedure within the dispute. His dialogical approach allows challenges to norms of recognition and
to the procedures through which those norms are negotiated; reasonable disagreement need not disappear after a settlement
(Tully, 2006, pp. 24–27). This supplies a more demanding interpretation of revision than an amendment field. A person can
have a route to appeal while being forbidden to challenge the categories that made the decision possible. The reconstruction

## Page 44

should distinguish correcting a mistaken classification, changing its consequences, revising the classification rule and contesting
the institution’s authority to impose that rule. Success at one level cannot be presumed to settle the others.
Non-finality does not require every decision to wait for unanimity. Tully distinguishes dialogue from decision-making and
retains other institutions as checks where negotiation leaves minorities vulnerable. He also asks whether ostensibly inclusive
negotiations are governed at a distance through their agendas and resource inequalities ( Tully, 2006, pp. 29–31). Our response
must therefore specify how an act can bind provisionally while its basis remains contestable. A decision could have an effective
date, a review trigger and continuing protective duties without declaring that participation ratifies the forum’s entire jurisdiction.
Equally, repeated negotiation should not allow those who control essential resources to postpone relief indefinitely. A timetable
distributes power when some parties can survive delay and others cannot.
National continuity and public contestation need different guarantees. A nation may sustain language, institutions and a
claim to self-government across generations; a public may organize around one controversy and dissolve when its work changes.
Giving each public a constituent veto would allow organizational multiplication to reorder the federation without a defended rule.
Giving only established nations agenda rights would let inherited recognition suppress emerging opposition. A possible division
would protect collective self-government through specified competences while securing direct channels for people contesting their
exercise. Its legitimacy would depend on the relation between the two, including who may reopen their boundaries. Neither
the age of an institution nor the novelty of its challengers determines that answer.
In the hypothetical drought, this would separate three questions currently liable to collapse. Workers need drinking water
and a practicable role in decisions affecting their lives. A Treaty Nation may contest the watershed body’s title to allocate
water. Residents may challenge an upstream association’s control of storage while declining membership in it. These claims
concern intersecting material relations but do not carry identical warrants. A temporary provision agreement should identify
who undertakes the duty, who funds it, what participation authorizes and which jurisdictional claims remain unsettled. The
preservation of a claim cannot itself supply water; the supply of water cannot silently extinguish the claim.
Remaining together consequently requires more than portable standing and toleration of disagreement. Participants need
means to develop opposition, powers through which it can affect decisions and routes to contest the procedures that distribute
those powers. They may continue to disagree about the federation’s story, their own representative institutions and the proper
source of common authority. Composition without capture names a defensible task only if these disagreements can alter the
arrangement while people retain the conditions of participation. Its political value lies in exposing the points where such
alteration is blocked, including by the reconstruction’s own account of reconciliation.

Xeno-California comprises five hypothetical conflicts over territory, cross-border aﬀiliation, new publics, civic rank and scarce
water. Each has two machine-readable alternatives with stipulated differences in institutional design. Land, labor, infrastructure,
power and affected nonmembers remain part of the decision being compared.
Case A places several nations and unaﬀiliated residents in one territory. The bounded alternative grounds common services
and ordinary political standing in personhood and residence while permitting language and cultural institutions. The capture
alternative requires one exclusive national registration to obtain those services. The relevant evidence in a future study would
include effective access, internal dissent and representation, not the number of recognized organizations.
Case B places one national community across jurisdictions. A limited cultural agreement can coordinate institutions without
moving borders or licensing universal control over co-nationals. The capture alternative treats the association’s national claim
as authority over every aﬀiliate everywhere. The issuer’s level, the agreement’s reach and the body able to enforce it must be
separately specified. Territorial institutions retain duties to residents who reject the cultural organization.
Case C asks how a new generation becomes a public. Recognition need not require common descent or imitation of a constitu-
tional list of old nations. But a new public’s cultural claims do not automatically generate taxing, policing or secession powers.
The rejected alternative infers coercive competence from an asserted eternal-state interpretation. A future mechanism must
distinguish opportunities to organize, public recognition, representation and authorized powers.
Case D refuses a civic hierarchy of vision. Oﬀicials who classify a community as fourfold cannot infer that it deserves leadership
over a supposedly single-vision community. Changing from three to four or six interpretive lenses in the prototype leaves
admissibility unchanged. That software control establishes only the absence of a programmed lens-count entitlement; it does
not validate any interpretation of fourfold vision.
Case E involves a wealthy national association seeking fiscal autonomy, an Indigenous community with land and water claims,
migrant labor without association membership, and downstream affected residents. A meaningful alternative identifies duties,

8.1 Shared evidence, reliable reports and the authority to act
Federated inference specifies how partial observers can learn through one another. Friston and colleagues model sentinels with
complementary views of a shared environment. Their communications carry beliefs and confidence through likelihood mappings;

an agent’s own message and transmits predictions grounded in earlier observations [ Friston et al. (2024), sec. 3.1, Eqs. 6–8;
Figs. 3–5]. The task’s latent variables describe a shared environment; poetic-state interpretations retain their own type in
the present model. Translation therefore involves decisions about coordinates, categories, confidence and the independence of
reports. A common description acquires value through the perspectives it makes available.
The categorical framework in Robust Belief Sharing separates local robust updating from server aggregation. It distinguishes
a fixed-weight logarithmic pool, divergence-based heuristic reweighting, and a separate objective-backed variational aggregator.
Its zero-robustness identity returns the project’s log-linear pool. Under finite positive shared support, posterior-log-potential
representations and fixed weights, that pool specializes Friston and colleagues’ message-combination term; recovery of the

11.9 Production, design and interpretive authority
Composition without capture must constrain the scholar who reconstructs it. An interpretation can give every person several
aﬀiliations yet reserve to its author the power to decide what those aﬀiliations ultimately mean. Its generosity then depends
on a monopoly over the common description. The question is whether a participant can contest the account of humanity
through which inclusion is offered while continuing to share institutions, resources and obligations. A defensible reconstruction
must allow disagreement with its poetic vocabulary to count as political argument. Otherwise, passage between states remains
available only within a world whose interpretive boundaries someone else has fixed.
Viscomi supplies a methodological restraint on this authority. His account of the workshop makes production part of meaning
and resists both excusing execution in the name of vision and decoding every mark as a symbol. Decoration can organize
attention while also serving the practical requirements of etching and inking ( Viscomi, 1982, author reprint web pp. 3–8,
compilation PDF pp. 60–65). The implication here is that neither an attractive political reading nor an exact transcription
exhausts an illuminated page. A claim about reciprocity needs an account of dramatic speech and visual relations; a claim
about a particular mark needs alternatives that include medium and execution. Interpretation becomes more answerable when
it identifies what could defeat it.
Essick and Viscomi make the material disagreement specific. Their inquiry distinguishes monochrome, hand-colored and color-
printed impressions and argues against a general two-pull account. They interpret the exceptional second printing of Nurses
Song in copy E as repair ( Essick and Viscomi , 2002, pp. 74–77, 98–102). The physical history assigned to a feature changes
what an interpretation can infer from it. Their technical conclusion is an attributed argument; the selected opening and ending
supply this comparison, rather than an independent resolution of the controversy.
Ritchie’s account of the Blake–Stedman engravings makes reproduction part of the interpretive problem. Her discussion of
contemporary reworkings ends by questioning the authority carried forward when historical images circulate, even under a
critical or emancipatory description ( Ritchie, 2025, paras. 26–30). Her objects have a different history from the Jerusalem
sheets shown here; the methodological demand concerns how an image is made to support a claim. The captions in this
article identify copy and sheet, describe a visible feature, locate the selected passage and name the critical purpose. Original
diagrams sit alongside those impressions as the article’s constructions. A plate can complicate an argument without certifying
its institutional conclusion.
The figures work at three levels. A transcription supplies the selected wording, an identified impression makes a material page
available for criticism, and an analytical diagram displays a proposed or measured relation. Captions identify the object, the
evidence it carries and the question it addresses. Copy sensitivity therefore exceeds duplicated words. Deduplication cannot
decide which material differences matter; equal-family weighting cannot supply a comparison of pages. Each calculation can
expose a choice that further reading and inspection must examine.
11.10 When would computation add something?
A typed relational graph can separate persons, aﬀiliations, interpretations and competencies. Multiplex relations can coexist
without transferring authority across their layers. A model can expose a missing warrant or prohibited identification, but
cannot create a political right by drawing an edge. A richer implementation should begin with a decision, rival account and
independently justified norm before a compositional or active-inference formalism. A federated extension can hold the dispute
and safeguards fixed while varying communication access, misleading reports and the ability to challenge a classification. It
should record inference quality and practical contestability separately. The technical sources specify shared representations,
message construction and aggregation checks; the institutional record specifies who may impose the consequence and how an
affected person can obtain revision ( Friston et al. , 2024, secs. 3.1–3.2; Friedman, 2026c, secs. 5.8–5.9, 7.5).
An ablation is especially important. Remove poetic-state descriptors while holding safeguards and material circumstances
fixed. If the useful distinctions remain, institutional novelty belongs to political comparators, while Blake supplies critical or
interpretive value. If imaginative description makes a difference, identify how: whose reasons become audible, what assumption
changes and whether this is possible without compelled metaphysical assent. Current invariance under lens count is one small
software control, not that entire empirical comparison.
