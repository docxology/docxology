Abstract
How do Blake’s small lives make another being’s needs matter? This article argues that provision, feeding and aggregation give
different forms to that question: help can enable another life, appetite can use it, and a collective body can redistribute its
powers. Copy-specific readings of four short poems, nine selected historical scans and bounded prophetic and prose passages
test this account against symbolic, spiritual and human-centered alternatives. The emmet’s offered route, the fly’s reciprocal
likeness and the Worm/infant comparison become visual arguments in which the complete impression remains beside the exact
words, a rival reading and a limit on biological identification. A reproducible concordance supplies breadth without turning
retrieval into a bestiary: 102 categories yield 36,039 category-source-span candidates in 2,067 captured units; 2,498 belong to
199 identified Blake-primary reading units. Within that scope, 82 name-family categories supply 1,239 candidates. Pooled
and equal nonempty-group compositions differ by total variation 0.222387 across 26 positive groups. Source-role contrasts,
finite proximity experiments and recorded reading windows expose dependence on particular inputs. The contextual ledger
contains 47 authored decisions and leaves 35,992 candidates unreviewed. These measures describe a search and its selective
examination, not animal prevalence or complete-oeuvre coverage. Baine’s biological-symbolic scholarship, accessed here through
a contemporary review, makes contextual variability a continuing challenge. The argument locates ethical force in particular
acts of hearing, sustaining, injuring and joining; neither a shared name nor a modern lineage determines that force.
1 Small lives and the work of attention
The emmet’s diﬀiculty in “A Dream” is practical: she is lost, tired and separated from her family. The poem makes that diﬀiculty
audible before a glow-worm offers light and a beetle’s movement offers a route home. The speaker contributes sympathy, but
does not perform the entire rescue. Distributed capacities become useful because they respond to a particular creature’s need.
The animal scene therefore gives this article a question more precise than whether Blake likes insects: what forms of attention
and association make another being’s life possible? The reading concerns “A Dream,” E16, checked against retained Erdman
record 26 and Archive Songs copy A, object 27, especially physical text lines 5, 16 and 20–21. Archive source.
“Entomology” names the inquiry rather than restricting it to the modern class Insecta. Worms, spiders, centipedes, scorpions,
snails and Polypus belong within the requested field. Their differences matter. A glow-worm’s compound name must not turn
it into an earthworm, and a mythic Polypus must not acquire a modern species identity through a database field. The project
records Blake’s expression first and biological classification, when warranted, separately. Ambiguous boundaries between animal,
vegetable, human and collective life can themselves be literary evidence.
This breadth also prevents a familiar selection bias. Reading only the short lyrics privileges the fly, worm and emmet while
overlooking prophetic inventories, prose, manuscripts, annotations and visual descriptions. Reading all source planes together
creates the opposite error: a curator’s image description or editorial note can become a false Blake quotation. The present
method retrieves broadly while preserving the distinction between a captured lead and an admitted interpretive claim.
The argument follows three relations rather than a stable bestiary: provision that enables another life, feeding that uses a
living body, and aggregation that reorganizes its members’ powers. A glow-worm’s assistance and the Clay’s nurture make
dependence answerable; a worm’s destructive intimacy and the wine-press’s suffering prevent abundance from guaranteeing
reciprocity. The Polypus asks what happens to distinct capacities when lives join. Each relation can intersect the others, but
neither a shared animal name nor a biological analogy supplies a single moral verdict.
2 Biological symbolism and its critical inheritances
Rodney M. Baine’s The Scattered Portions: William Blake’s Biological Symbolism , prepared with the assistance of Mary R.
Baine, is a necessary book-length predecessor. Its insect chapter is described in an inspected contemporary review; the chapter
itself was unavailable for direct reading in this research pass. Its formal heading and boundaries therefore remain unverified.
(Baine, 1986; Hoagwood, 1989, p. 40)
Hoagwood values Baine’s historical sources, examples and illustrations while questioning the relation between symbolic classifi-
cation and context. His insect discussion reports an interpretation of metamorphosis through individuals and states, alongside
spiders associated with institutional entrapment. The review also recognizes Baine’s acknowledgment of variable meanings. Its
objection is more demanding than a rejection of symbolism: a particular passage or design may hold contrary implications
together instead of assigning them to separate moral categories. This account belongs to Hoagwood’s review, not to a direct
reading of Baine’s chapter. ( Hoagwood, 1989, pp. 39–42)
The strongest rival to the present argument follows from that problem. Provision, feeding and aggregation might all stage
human faculties, spiritual states or regeneration. Calling them relations does not make them cease to be symbols; recording

Gould, Baker and Erasmus Darwin offer historically available comparisons, with no demonstrated reading or influence chain
from these books to Blake. ( Perkins, 1999; Srigley, 1992; Essick and Viscomi , 2002; Fara, 2020)
The contribution attempted here is a reproducible relation between broad retrieval and situated reading. Every retrieved
lead should be traceable to its source span and edition. Submitted contextual decisions additionally retain their reviewer and
rationale; a close reading in this article does not create a decision in that separate ledger. Every declared search form should
remain inspectable, including zero-result forms and noisy expansions. This is useful precisely where a polished argument might
otherwise hide its selection of examples. The method does not remove interpretive judgment; it makes the decisions through
which a concordance becomes an argument available for examination.
3 From a captured expression to a literary claim
The corpus is an independently copied, private application bundle exported by the public blake repository. Its pin identifies
the captured manifest, recovered projection, SQLite index and portable bundle manifest separately. Original passage bodies,
source hashes, author identities, edition/copy fields, locators, rights and roles survive export. Export verifies the consumed
input bytes before and after the copy. A Git repository’s code, a source capture and an application’s result epoch remain
separate objects.
The baseline searches 2,067 captured units: 540 reading documents, 796 nonempty Erdman pages and 731 raw XML units.
Of the reading documents, 199 carry the identified Blake author and primary role; 341 remain unverified. The Erdman pages
and four XML parts are mixed/editorial source material. The 727 Archive XML documents remain unverified as whole units
because they combine text, apparatus, metadata and descriptions. These counts describe source units, not 2,067 distinct works
or independent historical witnesses. The counts and identities are bound in research/corpus-pin.json and the text-free
baseline summary.
The initial vocabulary has 102 categories and 376 explicit forms. Historical forms include Emmet and Catterpiller. The
user-specified emmett is an explicit candidate spelling, not silently asserted as an attested Blake form. Compound families
include earth-worm, tape-worm, glow-worm, silk-worm, honey bee and cock chafer. The implementation uses the existing
Blake Unicode normalization and retains original character offsets. Whole-token matching prevents ant from matching giant
and bee from matching been. It does not silently stem fly into every motion verb.
Separate contextual categories include web, silk, honey, hive, shell, wing, swarm, sting and spinning. These help recover
discussion that does not name an animal, but they introduce many unrelated cases. A wing can belong to an angel or bird; a
spinner can be a human worker; a sting can belong to a plant. Such forms cannot supply an insect total without contextual
review.
The engine emits every category/source/span candidate without a display cap, plus all nine role/plane cells for every category,
including zeros. Equivalent forms in one category share an identical source span; different categories may overlap. Thus
honey bee can yield a compound candidate and a bee candidate. Neither category sums nor repeated printed copies become
distinct animal observations. Exact-text grouping is reported separately and does not establish that nonidentical editions are
independent witnesses.

3.1 Words, designs and the material page
An animal word and a depicted animal are different kinds of evidence. A transcription can establish that a poem names a fly
without establishing that a particular mark in its design depicts one. Conversely, a visually prominent body can matter without
supplying an additional lexical occurrence. The illuminated page makes this distinction productive: lettering, vegetation, figures
and coloured grounds guide the movement through the poem, sometimes offering an analogy and sometimes withholding the
object that a reader expects to find. The relevant question is therefore not simply whether the picture confirms the text, but
what each medium enables the reader to notice, and what remains uncertain after they have been considered together.
Material scholarship also discourages treating colour as a transparent record of a single original conception. Essick and Viscomi
distinguish impressions printed in one ink and subsequently coloured from colour printing, in which colour is transferred from
the plate. They also show how cropping, straightening and mounting can conceal the relation between an impression and its
sheet. Their argument concerns printing processes and bibliographical evidence; it does not license assigning an intention to
each difference between two digital reproductions. ( Essick and Viscomi , 2002, pp. 74–76, 91–92) Here the complete captured
scans remain available beside the resized figures. Details are enlarged only with their crop boundaries recorded. None has been
rotated or recoloured.
The distinction matters particularly for small lives. Enlarging a minute shape may make its outlines easier to inspect while
also detaching it from the proportions and verbal setting through which it becomes meaningful. The four Songs impressions
reproduced here belong to copy Y at the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Their text is checked in that copy; they do not inherit the
copy-A reading credit of the earlier casebook. The Thel comparison likewise names each physical witness. This is an addition
of seven selected visual witnesses, not a representative sample of all copies or a visual census of Blake’s animals. ( Blake, 1825,
1795, 1818)
3.2 Historical names and conditional classification
The modern reference contains 37 admitted NCBI taxon records and an explicit mapping policy for all 102 historical search
categories. Each admitted name, rank and ancestry is checked against two retained EFetch responses, identified by retrieval
URL, access date and SHA-256. The schematic compresses omitted ancestors while preserving captured ancestry. Its layout
comes from the shared validated evidence-tree API. This operation uses no sequence alignment, evolutionary model or dating
analysis. NCBI explains that its classification uses literature-based curation and that its Common Tree is an abbreviated
classification display. These captured records are a versioned reference, subject to revision, rather than a final nomenclatural
authority. ( National Center for Biotechnology Information , n.d.; National Library of Medicine , n.d.)

Conditional modern animal senses connect fly to Diptera, spider to Araneae, ant/emmet to Formicidae and bee to Anthophila.
These are reference lineages, not decisions that every retrieved token refers to an organism in that lineage. Anthophila is
more selective than treating all Apoidea as bees. The explicit emmett and emmitt query expansions remain unattested in
these captured matches; they have not been erased from the old registry. The old insect retrieval group for larva is retained
historically, while the mapping policy marks the life-stage term as unresolved across animal groups. A query tag is not a
biological classification.
The five mapping states distinguish a resolved reference lineage, a broad group, an ambiguous mapping, a non-taxon lead and
an outside-scope label. Worm is a polyphyletic literary term whose possible animal senses cross annelids, flatworms, nematodes
and insect forms; these examples are not exhaustive. Glow-worm cannot be assigned to Annelida by its final word. Blindworm
may fall outside the invertebrate scope. Polypus, zoophyte and animalcule retain historical and figurative uncertainty rather
than acquiring species names. Parts, products and actions such as wing, silk, honey and spin receive no taxon target. No
category total is allocated to a taxon by this policy, and “invertebrates” is not presented as a clade.
The lineage diagram is one side of a three-part inquiry: identify the printed expression, read its local use, and only then consider
a modern classification. These relations are neither a linguistic family tree nor a history of influence. Similar spelling can join
unlike bodies; unlike words can name an animal family. The opening visual arguments make these distinctions concrete in the
same material witnesses used for their quotations.

4.1 A Dream: assistance without a completed rescue
“A Dream” makes homecoming depend on several kinds of agency. The emmet speaks her own distress; the dreamer hears
it; the glow-worm can illuminate the ground; the beetle provides an audible orientation. On the reading proposed here, the
poem’s coordination is organized around an unresolved need. The closing imperative offers a route home; it does not narrate a
completed rescue. The glow-worm’s oﬀice as watchman also leaves an alternative reading through assigned social roles. Both
readings must account for the sequence of distress, the speaker’s tear, practical advice and the ending. This is an interpretive
proposal about the poem’s sequence, not a biological claim about ant navigation or a measured social network. The glow-worm’s
familiar association with illumination is insuﬀicient by itself: what matters is when and for whom its capacity becomes useful.
In copy Y’s “A Dream,” orange-brown lettering occupies almost the entire designed field, while curling stems, tufts and leaves
pass around and between its stanzas. Blue washes gather along parts of the margins. The reader’s route through speech is also
a route through this vegetation. A small upright, pale figure at the lower right becomes clearer in a bounded enlargement, but
its identity is less secure than the text’s naming of the glow-worm and beetle. It would be misleading to turn every adjacent
mark into an insect. What can be seen with greater confidence is a page that makes the written journey dense with branching
and interlacing forms. [ Blake (1825), plate 26, object 26, physical lines 3–22; Met 17.10.26]
William Gould’s An Account of English Ants supplies a historically available contrast, without establishing that Blake read this
book. At the beginning of chapter VIII, Gould presents ant labour as an example of public care and an admonition to human
indolence. But that moral use coexists with a demand for repeated observation. Later in the chapter he disputes inherited

4.2 The Fly: consolation after the creature’s injury
“The Fly” makes another relation possible through the thoughtlessness of a human hand. Its human/animal comparison can
support sympathy, but the comparison also raises a diﬀiculty: does the speaker attend to the fly’s life or use it to think about
his own mortality? The poem’s alternating questions give reason to retain both possibilities. A review record should therefore
distinguish an explicit fly reference from its rhetorical function and from the reader’s judgment about the speaker. The source
anchor is Archive Songs copy A, object 33; the retained Erdman text begins at E23, record 33. In this advancement the complete
copy-A physical transcription, lines 3–22, was read: the final happiness follows an explicit conditional relation between thought
and life. It is the speaker’s conclusion, not testimony from the fly about its injury. Full-poem transcription credit does not
include the design, other copies or all of Songs. ( Blake, n.d.e, object 33, physical lines 3–22)

Michael Srigley supplies a substantial counterreading to a straightforward encounter with an insect. He connects the nocturnal
worm to traditions of spectral beings and to Paracelsian explanations of illnesses produced by disordered imagination. On his

because the worm’s available senses are being made unstable. A literal animal reading must explain the secret love; a sexual

a code with one expendable noun. Their second-person address also matters: the rose is told of its sickness and destruction,
but does not answer. The reader hears an attribution of harm rather than an exchange in which both parties describe their
relation.
4.4 The Human Abstract: pity and the nourishment of cruelty
The caterpillar and fly of “The Human Abstract” complicate a uniformly tender reading. They feed within an account of fear,
cruelty and mystery that ends in the human brain. A contextual annotation must preserve the predication and setting rather
than importing the sympathy of “A Dream” because both passages contain insects. Reading the complete transcription in this
pass makes the final location of the sought tree in the human brain part of the feeding scene’s context. The caterpillar and fly
therefore cannot supply an empirical plant-host relation through their nouns alone. Source: Songs copy A, object 41, physical

“The Human Abstract” resists the opposite simplification, in which insects always stand for persecuted innocence. Its caterpillar
and fly feed on Mystery after Cruelty has prepared a snare, watered the ground and brought forth rooted Humility. The final

The opening attack on pity that depends upon making another person poor prevents compassion from remaining an unexamined
virtue. Read beside “A Dream,” it asks whether assistance answers a prior need or benefits from maintaining dependence. The
feeding insects are implicated in a growth whose nourishment is morally compromised; they do not independently explain its
origin. This distinction keeps appetite from becoming either automatic guilt or automatic innocence. What matters is the
relation between feeding, the conditions that sustain it, and the voice that makes those conditions intelligible.

6.3 Webs, prisons and the allocation of danger
The prison towers of The French Revolution make it diﬀicult to attach one moral value to a creature or its structure. Within the
selected passage, a man is confined for prophetic writing, another has refused to sign papers of abhorrence, and a woman has
resisted a minister. The named towers assign institutional categories to persons whose acts the narrative also preserves. The
old man in the tower called Order occupies a den as narrow as a child’s grave, among webs and the slime of ancient horrors. The
surrounding snakes and scorpions, however, are described as harmless companions sharing his sorrowful breath. The grammar
does not extend that description automatically to every spider. It nevertheless prevents the tower’s animal occupants from
simply bearing the blame for its confinement. ( Blake, n.d.b, bounded Archive reading windows E29-17 and E29-27)
This is a local test of the relation between biological symbolism and institutional entrapment reported in Hoagwood’s review of
Baine. A web can make the prison’s accumulated enclosure visible without making a spider the author of the prison. Conversely,
retaining an animal sense does not turn the webs into a natural-history observation. Material habitation, political analogy and
the narrator’s allocation of suffering coexist. The adjacent eternal worm in the tower Bloody introduces another relation to
the confined body; its eschatological and bodily senses remain unresolved in the existing uncertain decision. The two retrieved
categories therefore lead into overlapping context, but neither their proximity nor their shared setting makes them one symbolic
function. ( Hoagwood, 1989, pp. 40–42)
The distinction matters beside the practical assistance in “A Dream” and the joined body of Milton. Association can answer
distress, accompany it without remedy, or reorganize the powers of those joined. The old man’s companions do not release him;
their harmlessness cannot establish the harmlessness of Order. Nor does the glow-worm’s oﬀice establish that every assigned
social place enables another life. What an association does must be read in its sequence and distribution of powers. This
comparison proposes no equivalence between these poetic institutions and a modern constitutional procedure, and it does not
resolve the unread Baine chapter. Its narrower result is that naming a web, finding an animal sense and identifying the agent
of an injury are different interpretive operations.

7.2 Senses, cells and the limits of emancipatory speech
In Visions of the Daughters of Albion , the question “With what sense does the bee form cells?” belongs to a sequence that
differentiates creatures by their habitations, pursuits and pleasures. Oothoon’s questions move through the chicken and pigeon,
bee, mouse and frog before the wild ass, camel and other beings. Shared organs do not provide a suﬀicient explanation of their
different activities. The sequence ends by making that inquiry prior to an account of human thought. The bee is neither a
specimen whose behaviour has been measured nor a replaceable decorative comparison. Its cell-making contributes a distinct
activity to an argument about the limits of a uniform account of sensing. ( Blake, 1793a, object 5, physical lines 3–14)
Hutchings reads this differentiated animal activity as a condition of human self-reflection and relates it to the poem’s challenge
to imperial uniformity. His comparison with Swedenborg distinguishes compelled behaviour from Blake’s language of creaturely
pleasure; the cited Swedenborg and Raine texts have not been independently read here. His later objection is equally consequen-
tial: Oothoon’s apparently generous sexual vision can reproduce capture and the appropriation of other people. Emancipatory
speech does not secure its own emancipatory effects. These are claims from selected paragraphs of Hutchings’s essay, not a
reading of his monograph or the complete essay. ( Hutchings, 2001, paragraphs 19–22)
For this article, the consequence is a test of who benefits from the analogy. The bee question opens a space for capacities
that the human observer cannot explain through an inventory of organs alone. Yet the next lines return to Oothoon’s desire
for Theotormon’s attention, before he begins his own questions. The passage’s sequence keeps an argument about animal
difference beside a specific, unanswered human address. ( Blake, 1793a, object 5, physical lines 15–26) This local transition does

supplies a separate, explicitly coded case of connective material proposed for capture. The present transcription reading creates
no new candidate decision, inspected image or complete-work credit.
7.3 Animal suffering and the force of an augury
“Auguries of Innocence” poses a further diﬀiculty: what should follow when injury has been noticed? Perkins argues that its
animal auguries voice innocence confronting cruelty and threatening consequences that exceed ordinary causal explanation. He
also warns that treating a tormented creature solely as a metaphor can diminish attention to the animal’s suffering. These are
interpretive positions, not an uncontested identification of the poem’s voice. ( Perkins, 1999, pp. 7–10) They encourage keeping
literal injury present while examining the form in which a speaker makes it consequential.
The boy who kills the fly is threatened with the spider’s enmity. This is not simply a food-chain statement: the two-line address
assigns a consequence to a human act. Yet the creature associated with catching flies becomes the figure through which killing a
fly is answered. Elsewhere the caterpillar repeats maternal grief, and the prohibition on killing moth or butterfly is attached to
judgment. [ Blake (n.d.a), E490–491, records 502–503; “Auguries,” lines 33–40] The sequence crosses animal behaviour, familial
feeling and eschatological warning without making them interchangeable. A consistently gentle insect world would have no
place for the spider’s enmity; a purely predatory one would lose the address to the boy. The poems considered here make small
lives matter through those unresolved relations between attention, use, obligation and response. Their ethical force depends on
retaining the differences that a single animal-symbol key would erase.
The sequence also includes gnat, bee, cricket and emmet, but a lexical resemblance need not name an animal. The Mite at
E492 is an amount extracted from labour, not an arachnid. Broad retrieval preserves that negative control. Its retained locator
is record 504; this expansion adds no new reading credit for that page. The distinction between use, deliberate cruelty and
metaphor requires a speaker, a predication and a setting, not merely a noun. ( Blake, n.d.a)
